# ESVP Retrospective Exercise: Explorer, Shopper, Vacationer, Prisoner

Before diving into sticky notes and action items, smart facilitators check the room. Not the physical room — the emotional one. Is the team engaged or going through the motions? Eager to explore or waiting for the meeting to end?

The ESVP exercise answers this question in under ten minutes. It reveals how each team member approaches the retrospective before any substantive discussion begins. That insight shapes everything that follows.

ESVP stands for **Explorer, Shopper, Vacationer, and Prisoner** — four mindsets that describe how people show up to retrospectives and similar team meetings. Understanding these mindsets helps facilitators adapt their approach and helps teams address engagement problems at the source.

## What Is the ESVP Exercise?

The ESVP exercise is a brief retrospective warmup attributed to Norman Kerth, author of *Project Retrospectives*. Participants anonymously select the role that best describes how they feel about the upcoming retrospective:

| Role | Symbol | Mindset | Description |
|------|--------|---------|-------------|
| **Explorer** | 🔍 | Eager and curious | "I am fully engaged and want to discover new insights" |
| **Shopper** | 🛒 | Selective interest | "I will take away one or two useful ideas" |
| **Vacationer** | 🏖️ | Relaxed detachment | "I am happy to be here instead of working, but not deeply invested" |
| **Prisoner** | ⛓️ | Reluctant attendance | "I feel forced to be here and would rather be elsewhere" |

Each person picks the role that resonates — not the role they think they should pick. Results are collected anonymously, tallied, and shared with the group.

The exercise is diagnostic, not judgmental. It does not label people. It surfaces the collective mood so the facilitator can respond appropriately.

## Why ESVP Matters

Retrospectives fail when participants are disengaged. A facilitator running a thoughtful [Start Stop Continue](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/start-stop-continue-retrospective-template) session with a room full of Prisoners produces silence, sarcasm, or performative participation.

ESVP gives you data before you invest 60 minutes in a format that will not work. It answers:

- Should I adjust the format based on energy levels?
- Is there a systemic engagement problem to address?
- Do I need to spend extra time on safety before gathering data?

Teams that run ESVP regularly track engagement trends over time. A sprint with mostly Explorers followed by a sprint with mostly Vacationers signals something changed — burnout, a difficult release, leadership changes, or retrospective fatigue.

## How to Run the ESVP Exercise

The exercise takes 10–15 minutes. Run it at the start of a retrospective, after setting the stage but before the main activity.

### Step 1: Introduce the Four Roles (3 minutes)

Display the four roles with brief descriptions. Read each one aloud. Emphasize:

- There are no right or wrong answers
- Responses are anonymous
- Pick the role that honestly describes how you feel right now, not how you want to feel

### Step 2: Collect Responses Anonymously (2 minutes)

Each team member selects their role. Methods:

**In person:**
- Four corners of the room labeled E, S, V, P — but this reveals choices, so prefer anonymous methods
- Folded paper ballots collected in a box
- Sticky notes placed face-down, shuffled, then revealed

**Remote:**
- Anonymous poll in your retrospective tool
- Private message to the facilitator
- [SprintsPlans](https://sprintsplans.com) supports anonymous input, making ESVP collection seamless for distributed teams

Anonymity is critical. Without it, Prisoners will not admit they are Prisoners, and the exercise loses its value.

### Step 3: Tally and Share Results (2 minutes)

Count the responses. Display the distribution:

```
Explorer:    ████████  4
Shopper:     ████      2
Vacationer:  ██        1
Prisoner:    ██        1
```

Share the results without identifying individuals. The team sees the collective mood, not who feels what.

### Step 4: Discuss Briefly (5–8 minutes)

Ask the group:

- "What do these results tell us about how we are showing up today?"
- "Is there anything we should adjust before continuing?"
- "If there are Prisoners, what would make this retrospective more worthwhile for you?"

**Important:** Do not pressure Prisoners to explain themselves. The discussion is about the team's collective engagement, not individual interrogation. If Prisoner count is high, the facilitator should ask what would make retrospectives more valuable — and take that feedback seriously.

### Step 5: Adapt and Continue

Based on results, adjust the retrospective:

| Result Pattern | Facilitator Response |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Mostly Explorers | Proceed with planned format; team is ready |
| Mix of Explorers and Shoppers | Normal; proceed with standard format |
| High Vacationers | Shorten the retro, use an engaging format like [Lean Coffee](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/lean-coffee-retrospective) |
| Any Prisoners | Acknowledge openly, ask what would help, consider shortening or changing format |
| Mostly Prisoners | Stop and address why retrospectives feel pointless before continuing |

## Understanding Each Role

### Explorer

Explorers are your ideal retrospective participants. They come curious, contribute actively, and leave with insights. When most of the team are Explorers, standard formats work well. Challenge them with deeper activities like the [timeline retrospective](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/timeline-retrospective-activity) or root cause analysis.

Explorers can become dominant voices. Watch for [facilitation balance](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/handle-silence-conflict-dominant-voices-retrospectives) even in an engaged room.

### Shopper

Shoppers are pragmatic participants. They will engage with ideas that seem useful and tune out the rest. This is healthy — not everyone needs to be passionate about process improvement.

To engage Shoppers, make the retrospective's value proposition clear: "Today we are focusing on the deployment issue that cost us six hours this sprint." Relevance converts Shoppers into active participants.

### Vacationer

Vacationers enjoy the break from regular work but are not invested in outcomes. They participate lightly — a few sticky notes, minimal discussion.

High Vacationer counts may indicate:

- Retrospective fatigue from repetitive formats
- A sprint where nothing notable happened
- Team burnout where the retro feels like one meeting too many
- Lack of follow-through on previous action items

Address Vacationer energy by rotating formats, shortening the session, or demonstrating that retrospective action items actually produce change.

### Prisoner

Prisoners feel trapped. They believe the retrospective is a waste of their time. Their disengagement is active — arms crossed, phone checking, sarcastic comments, or complete silence.

Prisoner mindsets often stem from:

- Repeated retrospectives with no follow-through on action items
- Retrospectives that became blame sessions
- Mandatory attendance without perceived value
- Managerial presence that suppresses honesty
- Retrospective formats that feel childish or pointless

When Prisoners appear, the facilitator's most important job is to listen. Ask: "What would make this time valuable for you?" Then act on the answer — even if that means ending the retrospective early and scheduling a conversation about why retrospectives are not working.

## ESVP as a Team Health Indicator

Run ESVP every three to four retrospectives — not every time, or it becomes rote. Track results over time:

| Sprint | E | S | V | P | Notes |
|--------|---|---|---|---|-------|
| 12 | 5 | 2 | 1 | 0 | Healthy engagement |
| 13 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 | Post-release fatigue |
| 14 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | Action items not followed through |
| 15 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 0 | Addressed follow-through issue |

Trends matter more than individual results. A single Prisoner is worth noting. Three Prisoners in consecutive retrospectives is a systemic problem requiring intervention.

Connect ESVP trends to your broader [team health checks](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/team-health-checks) and [continuous improvement system](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/continuous-improvement-system-for-teams).

## Facilitation Tips

### Never Reveal Individual Responses

The moment anonymity breaks, ESVP dies. Facilitators must protect individual choices absolutely.

### Do Not Shame Prisoners

"Why are you a Prisoner?" is the wrong question. "What would make retrospectives more valuable?" is the right one. [Accountability without blame](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/accountability-without-blame) applies here.

### Act on Feedback

If Prisoners say retrospectives never produce change, review your action item follow-through. [Turn retrospective feedback into action items](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/turn-retrospective-feedback-into-action-items) that the team actually completes.

### Do Not Overuse ESVP

Running ESVP every retrospective makes it a checkbox. Use it periodically or when you sense engagement shifting.

### Address Systemic Issues Outside the Retro

If ESVP consistently shows Prisoners and Vacationers, the problem may be organizational — mandatory meetings without empowerment, lack of psychological safety, or retrospective theater. Some issues need leadership conversations, not format changes.

## ESVP for Remote Teams

Remote teams face unique engagement challenges. Video fatigue, multitasking, and reduced social cues make Vacationer and Prisoner mindsets more common.

Remote ESVP best practices:

- Use anonymous digital polls, not raised hands on video
- Acknowledge video fatigue openly
- Keep retrospectives shorter when ESVP shows low engagement
- Use tools that support anonymous participation throughout the retro, not just during ESVP

[Psychological safety for remote teams](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/psychological-safety-remote-teams) is foundational. ESVP measures the symptom; safety work addresses the cause.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should ESVP results be recorded?

Yes, for trend tracking. Record counts only — never individual assignments. Review trends quarterly.

### What if everyone picks Explorer?

Unlikely but possible. Proceed with your planned format. Be aware that social desirability bias may inflate Explorer counts if anonymity was not protected.

### Can ESVP be used outside retrospectives?

Yes. Any team meeting where engagement matters — workshops, planning sessions, training — benefits from a quick engagement check.

### How do we reduce Prisoner counts?

Follow through on action items. Rotate formats. Demonstrate that retrospectives produce real change. Address blame dynamics. Make attendance genuinely valuable, not just mandatory.

### Is ESVP the same as a mood check?

Related but different. Mood checks capture emotional state. ESVP captures relationship to the meeting itself — are you here to explore, shop, vacation, or because you have no choice?

### Should managers see ESVP results?

The team sees aggregate results. Managers who are not part of the development team should not attend retrospectives at all. If a manager is a team member, they participate like everyone else — anonymously.

## Check Engagement Before You Facilitate

The ESVP exercise takes ten minutes and prevents sixty minutes of wasted retrospective time. It tells you whether the team is ready to explore, needs a format change, or has lost faith in the practice entirely.

Run it when engagement feels off. Run it periodically to track trends. Listen to what Prisoners and Vacationers tell you — not with defensiveness, but with curiosity about what would make retrospectives worth their time.

Explorers will always show up ready. The exercise exists for everyone else — and for the facilitator who needs to know which room they are actually standing in.
