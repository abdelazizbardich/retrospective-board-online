# How to Run Your First Sprint Retrospective

Your first sprint is done. You shipped something — maybe everything you planned, maybe less. The team is tired, relieved, or both. Someone mentions "the retro" and half the room nods while the other half wonders what that means.

A sprint retrospective is one of the most valuable events in Agile. It is where your team pauses to reflect on how you worked, celebrates what went well, surfaces problems honestly, and commits to specific improvements. Done well, it compounds: each retrospective makes the next sprint a little better.

Done poorly — or skipped entirely — teams repeat the same frustrations sprint after sprint without ever addressing root causes.

If you are facilitating your team's first retrospective, this guide walks you through everything you need: what to prepare, how to structure the meeting, which format to use, and how to make sure insights become action.

## What Is a Sprint Retrospective?

A sprint retrospective is a structured team meeting held at the end of each sprint. The Scrum Guide defines its purpose: inspect how the last sprint went regarding people, relationships, process, and tools — then identify and plan improvements.

It is not a blame session. It is not a status meeting. It is not optional, even when the sprint went perfectly.

The retrospective answers one question: **How can we work better together next sprint?**

For a deeper foundation, read our overview of [what a retrospective is](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/what-is-a-retrospective) and why it matters for continuous improvement.

## Before Your First Retrospective

Preparation separates a productive retrospective from an awkward hour of silence. Do this work before the meeting starts.

### Set Expectations with the Team

Many team members have never attended a retrospective. Explain:

- **Purpose** — Improve how we work, not judge individuals
- **Duration** — Typically 60–90 minutes for a two-week sprint
- **Confidentiality** — What is said in the room stays in the room
- **Outcome** — One to three concrete action items, not a list of complaints

Share the **retrospective prime directive**: regardless of what we discover, we understand and truly believe that everyone did the best job they could, given what they knew at the time, their skills and abilities, the resources available, and the situation at hand.

This framing reduces defensiveness and encourages honesty.

### Choose a Format

First retrospectives benefit from simple, familiar structures. Good starting options:

| Format | Best For | Complexity |
|--------|----------|------------|
| [Start Stop Continue](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/start-stop-continue-retrospective-template) | First-time teams | Low |
| [Mad Sad Glad](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/mad-sad-glad-retrospective) | Emotional check-in | Low |
| [4Ls (Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed For)](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/4ls-retrospective-template) | Balanced reflection | Low |
| Went Well / To Improve | Minimal structure | Very low |

Avoid complex formats for your first retro. You want the team focused on sharing, not deciphering instructions.

### Prepare Your Space

**In person:** A room with a whiteboard or wall space for sticky notes. Markers, sticky notes in multiple colors, a timer.

**Remote:** An online retrospective board. [SprintsPlans](https://sprintsplans.com) lets teams gather feedback, organize ideas, vote anonymously on priorities, and track action items — useful when your team is distributed or you want a record that persists beyond the meeting.

### Schedule It

Book the retrospective at the end of the sprint, after the sprint review. Allow 60 minutes minimum. Put it on the calendar as a recurring event so it becomes habit. See our guidance on [retrospective frequency and length](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/retrospective-frequency-and-length) for timing recommendations.

### Identify a Facilitator

The Scrum Master typically facilitates. If you do not have a Scrum Master, choose someone who can remain neutral, manage time, and encourage quiet voices. The facilitator participates but focuses on guiding the process.

## A 60-Minute Agenda for Your First Retrospective

This agenda works for most first-time teams. Adjust timing based on team size.

### 1. Set the Stage (5 minutes)

Welcome the team. State the purpose. Read or summarize the prime directive. Establish ground rules:

- One person speaks at a time
- No interrupting
- Critique process, not people
- All voices matter

Ask a quick check-in question: "In one word, how are you feeling about this sprint?" This warms up the room and gives you a read on team energy.

### 2. Gather Data (15 minutes)

Introduce your chosen format. For Start Stop Continue:

- **Start** — What should we begin doing?
- **Stop** — What should we stop doing?
- **Continue** — What is working that we should keep?

Give the team 10 minutes of silent writing (sticky notes or digital cards). Then have each person share their items briefly without discussion. Cluster similar notes together.

Silent writing first prevents [dominant voices](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/handle-silence-conflict-dominant-voices-retrospectives) from shaping the agenda before quieter teammates contribute.

### 3. Generate Insights (20 minutes)

Look at the clusters. Ask probing questions:

- What patterns do you see?
- Why did this keep happening?
- Is this a symptom of something deeper?

Use [root cause questions](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/sprint-retrospective-questions-root-causes) to move beyond surface complaints. "We missed the deadline" is a symptom. "We committed to too much because we fear saying no to stakeholders" is closer to a root cause.

The facilitator keeps discussion focused. Park unrelated topics in a "parking lot" for later.

### 4. Decide What to Do (15 minutes)

Choose one to three improvements for the next sprint. Criteria for good action items:

- **Specific** — "Improve communication" is vague. "Post daily blockers in the team channel by 10am" is actionable.
- **Owned** — One person (or pair) is responsible for each item.
- **Achievable** — The team can realistically do this in one sprint.
- **Measurable** — You can tell at the next retro whether it happened.

Vote on priorities if there are too many ideas. Anonymous voting — available in tools like SprintsPlans — helps teams surface what matters most without social pressure.

### 5. Close (5 minutes)

Summarize action items and owners. Ask for a closing reflection: "What is one thing you appreciated about this retrospective?" Thank the team. End on a constructive note.

Document action items where everyone can see them. You will review these at the start of the next retrospective.

## Facilitation Tips for First-Timers

### Handle Silence

Silence is normal in a first retrospective. People may be unsure what to share or worry about judgment. Techniques:

- Extend silent writing time
- Ask specific prompts: "What was the hardest moment this sprint?"
- Share your own observation first to model vulnerability
- Use anonymous input if the team seems hesitant

See more strategies for [handling silence, conflict, and dominant voices](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/handle-silence-conflict-dominant-voices-retrospectives).

### Keep It Safe

Psychological safety is the foundation of honest retrospectives. Signs it is working: people admit mistakes, disagree respectfully, and raise uncomfortable topics. Signs it is not: silence, jokes that deflect, or the same two people talking the entire time.

Remote teams face additional challenges. Our guide on [psychological safety for remote teams](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/psychological-safety-remote-teams) covers practical steps.

### Do Not Solve Everything

First retrospectives often surface months of accumulated frustration. You cannot fix it all in one meeting. Acknowledge themes, pick one to three actions, and commit to continuing the practice. Trust builds over multiple retrospectives.

### Timebox Ruthlessly

Discussion expands to fill available time. Use a visible timer. When a topic needs more exploration than the retro allows, create a separate follow-up meeting.

## After the Retrospective

The retrospective does not end when the meeting ends. Follow-through determines whether the practice sticks.

### Share Action Items

Post action items in your team channel, sprint board, or retrospective tool. Visibility creates accountability.

### Review at the Next Sprint Planning

Reference retrospective commitments during [sprint planning](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/sprint-planning-guide). If the team agreed to limit work in progress, factor that into the next sprint's capacity.

### Start the Next Retrospective with a Check-In

Before gathering new data, review previous action items:

- What did we commit to?
- What did we actually do?
- What got in the way?

Teams that skip this step lose faith in the retrospective. Why generate action items if nobody follows up?

### Rotate Formats

After two or three retrospectives with the same format, try something new. [Starfish](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/starfish-retrospective-template), [Sailboat](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/sailboat-retrospective-guide), and [Lean Coffee](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/lean-coffee-retrospective) keep engagement high. Our full [sprint retrospective guide](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/sprint-retrospective-guide) covers more options.

## Common First-Retrospective Mistakes

### Skipping It Because the Sprint Went Well

Good sprints contain learning too. What helped? How do we repeat it? Retrospectives after successful sprints reinforce positive behaviors.

### Letting It Become a Complaint Session

Venting without action breeds cynicism. The facilitator's job is to channel frustration into improvements. Use the "decide what to do" phase to create forward momentum.

### Inviting Stakeholders or Managers

The retrospective is for the development team. External attendees change the dynamic. Product feedback belongs in the [sprint review](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/sprint-review-vs-retrospective), not the retrospective.

### Generating Too Many Action Items

Three action items followed through beat ten action items forgotten. Quality over quantity.

### Not Documenting Outcomes

Without a record, insights disappear. Use a shared doc, board, or retrospective tool. [Turn retrospective feedback into action items](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/turn-retrospective-feedback-into-action-items) with clear owners and deadlines.

## Building a Retrospective Habit

One retrospective does not transform a team. A consistent practice does.

| Sprint | Focus |
|--------|-------|
| 1 | Establish safety and format |
| 2 | Follow up on first action items |
| 3 | Introduce a new format or deeper root cause analysis |
| 4+ | Integrate retrospectives into your [continuous improvement system](https://sprintsplans.com/blog/continuous-improvement-system-for-teams) |

Over time, the retrospective becomes the team's most trusted forum for honest conversation. Problems surface early. Wins get recognized. Improvement compounds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should our first retrospective be?

60 minutes is a good default for a two-week sprint with a team of five to eight people. Shorter sprints can use 45 minutes. Do not rush below 45 minutes — you need time for all five phases.

### What if our team is not doing Scrum?

Retrospectives are not exclusive to Scrum. Kanban teams, project teams, and leadership groups all benefit from regular reflection. Adapt the timing to your workflow.

### Do we need a Scrum Master to facilitate?

No. Any team member who can facilitate neutrally can run a retrospective. Consider rotating the facilitator role to build facilitation skills across the team.

### What if someone refuses to participate?

Do not force it. Model openness, protect safety, and give it time. Some people need to see that retrospectives are productive before they engage fully.

### Can we run retrospectives remotely?

Yes. Use a video call and an online board. Anonymous input and voting are especially valuable remotely. SprintsPlans supports distributed teams running retrospectives with feedback collection, anonymous voting, and action item tracking.

### How is this different from a post-mortem?

Post-mortems typically follow failures or project endings. Retrospectives happen regularly regardless of outcome and focus on continuous improvement, not incident analysis.

## Your First Retrospective Starts Now

Running your first sprint retrospective does not require expertise. It requires intention: a safe space, a simple format, honest conversation, and a few committed action items.

Prepare the room. Set the tone. Follow the agenda. Follow through on what you decide. Then do it again next sprint.

That rhythm — reflect, improve, deliver, reflect — is how good teams become great teams. Your first retrospective is the beginning of that cycle.
